Gearboxes rarely explode because somebody suddenly forgot how gears work. They fail because somebody stopped checking the oil.

Oil is boring. It doesn't make the machine faster. It doesn't add a new feature. Nobody launches a press release announcing that they have successfully lubricated a bearing.

It simply allows all the moving parts to continue talking to each other without grinding themselves into metal filings.

Companies have gearboxes too.

They are made up of order systems, billing systems, logistics systems, identity systems, retail stores, call centres and the people unfortunate enough to stand between them. When those systems stop communicating, the company may continue moving for a while—but every movement creates more heat, more friction and eventually more damage.

This brings me to Telstra.

## A Phone That Apparently Exists Everywhere Except in My Hand

About two months ago, I ordered a new iPhone from Telstra.

Around a month later, I received a message from StarTrack telling me that it had been delivered.

It had not.

Naturally, I panicked. An expensive phone had apparently been left somewhere, and I could not find it. I assumed it had been stolen.

It was Friday afternoon, so I called Telstra. They could not tell me what had happened. The weekend passed, and I eventually contacted StarTrack myself.

They told me the phone had been damaged in transit and returned to Telstra.

Okay. Annoying, but these things happen.

Phones get damaged. Trucks break down. Parcels fall off conveyor belts. This is why companies have insurance, replacement stock and processes for dealing with exceptions.

StarTrack told me that Telstra would contact me officially.

They didn't.

I contacted Telstra again. They acknowledged that something had happened, opened a case and told me I would receive official correspondence within five to seven business days.

That also didn't happen.

Then, while Telstra still had not replaced the phone or meaningfully acknowledged the situation, they ported the number from the previous provider.

The old service stopped working.

The new phone still did not exist.

We now had no functioning service on the old phone, no new phone and no ability to receive the two-factor authentication codes required to log into the Telstra account where we were apparently meant to resolve the problem.

That is quite an impressive little circle.

## Six Systems, No Shared Reality

Each Telstra system appeared to have its own version of events.

The ordering system believed the phone had been supplied.

The logistics system knew it had been returned.

The activation system decided it was time to port the number.

The billing system continued charging for the service.

The identity system wanted to send authentication codes to the number that had just been disconnected.

The support system apparently believed the phone needed to be "repaired."

Every one of these systems may have been behaving correctly according to its own narrow instructions.

Together, they were insane.

This isn't six different problems. It is one problem: Telstra has no shared version of reality.

A functioning system should know that a customer has never taken custody of a device. That fact should automatically stop billing, prevent the number from being ported, initiate a replacement and notify the customer.

Instead, every department continued marching forward as though the others did not exist.

This is what happens when you stop oiling the gearbox.

## The Store Full of Phones That Couldn't Give Me a Phone

Eventually, I went into a Telstra store.

I explained the situation and asked a fairly simple question:

You have iPhones here. I have paid for an iPhone. Telstra knows the original phone was damaged and returned before I received it. Can I walk out with one of the iPhones sitting in this store?

No.

Apparently, the system could not handle that.

I don't know exactly what kind of system Telstra has built, but when a national telecommunications company has a customer without a telephone standing inside a telephone store full of telephones—and the store cannot give that customer a telephone—the system is broken.

The physical solution was sitting a few metres away from me.

The administrative solution may as well have been on Mars.

After several phone calls, an online chat, a visit to the store and a complaint case, I still had not received any official explanation of what was happening.

## Frontline Workers Are Being Used as Shock Absorbers

Telstra regularly reminds customers not to abuse its staff.

And to be clear: people should not abuse frontline workers. The person answering the phone did not damage the parcel, design the billing system or decide to port the number.

But asking customers to remain calm is not a substitute for fixing the machinery creating their frustration.

A significant amount of anger aimed at frontline workers is manufactured upstream.

The company designs a system that cannot resolve obvious problems. It removes the worker's authority to make sensible decisions. It gives them incomplete information and ambiguous procedures. It then places that worker in front of an increasingly frustrated customer and asks them to absorb the impact.

The worker becomes a human shock absorber.

"Please don't abuse our staff" should be paired with:

"We will not design systems that force our staff to defend the indefensible."

Otherwise, the company is externalising the emotional cost of its own technical debt onto both the customer and the lowest-paid person in the chain.

## This Is Not a Company That Cannot Afford Oil

Telstra reported a profit of $2.343 billion for the 2025 financial year, an increase of 31 per cent from the previous year. It then reported another $1.205 billion in profit for the six months ending December 2025.[1]

This is not a company struggling to afford gearbox oil.

It has money.

The question is what it considers worthy of investment.

Maintenance is not glamorous. Replacing fragmented systems does not produce the same headline as launching an AI partnership, expanding 5G coverage or announcing a shiny new strategy.

But the basic machinery still matters.

There is no point building the world's most advanced network if a customer cannot receive a phone, access their account or speak to someone with enough authority to fix an obvious mistake.

Innovation without maintenance is simply adding horsepower to a vehicle with no oil.

## User Experience Is Becoming Infrastructure

We are entering a period where good user experience is no longer considered a luxury.

Startups have trained people to expect systems that communicate, update in real time and explain what is happening. Customers now know what good software feels like.

Large incumbent companies often mistake user experience for a nicer app, a new logo or a chatbot bolted onto an old process.

But user experience is not the colour of the button.

It is whether the ordering system knows what the logistics system knows. It is whether a customer has to explain the same story five times to five different people.

A beautiful interface sitting on top of contradictory systems is lipstick on a seized gearbox.

The people leading large technology-dependent organisations need to understand the core machinery of their businesses. They do not necessarily need to write the code, but they need to understand how information moves, where responsibility sits and what happens when something falls outside the happy path.

Otherwise, executives see improving dashboards while customers are standing in stores full of phones being told that no phone can be given to them.

## Oil the Gearbox

At the time of writing, I still do not have the phone.

I have been charged for a service attached to a device I never received. The old service was disconnected. I have been given promises, a case number and several different explanations—but no clear resolution.

My request is not complicated.

I want to finish the next phone call with either:

- A tracking number for a new phone; or
- Permission to collect one from a Telstra store.

That should not require an essay, an ombudsman complaint or several hours of my life.

But I am documenting it because this story is not really about one damaged iPhone.

It is about what happens when profitable companies stop maintaining the systems beneath them.

Eventually, the friction becomes visible.

Eventually, the workers and customers begin absorbing the heat.

Eventually, the gearbox seizes.

As Ice Cube says: check yourself before you wreck yourself.

Telstra, oil the gearbox.
